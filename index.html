MK-677
the ghrelin mimic

It's not a peptide and it's not an injection — MK-677 is a pill that pretends to be your hunger hormone, tricking the body into releasing more growth hormone. Studied for frailty, bone loss, and Alzheimer's, then shelved, and sold today only as an unregulated research chemical.

Quick read

MK-677 (ibutamoren) is a small-molecule drug — not a peptide — that mimics ghrelin, the "hunger hormone," at its receptor. That signal tells the pituitary to release growth hormone, which raises IGF-1. Human trials in the 1990s–2000s studied it for muscle wasting, hip fracture, and Alzheimer's; results were mixed and development stalled before approval. Reported effects include increased appetite, water retention, and higher blood sugar. It is banned in sport and sold only as an unregulated research chemical. Scroll for the full picture.

01The molecule

What MK-677 actually is

MK-677 (ibutamoren) is sold right alongside peptides — but it isn't one. It's a small-molecule drug: a single compact chemical, not a chain of amino acids. Everything interesting about it starts with that correction.

The single most misunderstood fact about MK-677: it was designed as a pill from day one.

Not actually a peptide

Peptides are chains of amino acids. MK-677 is a single small molecule — chemically closer to a conventional pill drug than to anything in a peptide vial.

A pill, not an injection

It's orally active: it survives the digestive tract and reaches the bloodstream from a capsule. That was the entire point of developing it — growth hormone itself can't do that.

A ghrelin impersonator

Its trick is mimicking ghrelin — the stomach hormone that signals hunger — at the ghrelin receptor, which also happens to trigger growth hormone release.

Plain English

Think of peptides as bead necklaces and MK-677 as a single marble. The marble is tiny enough to pass through the gut wall into the blood — something a bead necklace usually can't do. Same neighborhood (hormone signaling), completely different object.

02Hunger pill

The pill that mimics hunger

MK-677 began in the 1990s as a serious pharmaceutical project: a growth-hormone pill for frail older adults. The science was real, the trials happened — and then the program quietly died.

From pharma labs to forum lore: the science papers outlived the drug program.
The idea

Growth hormone injections work but are expensive and injectable. A pill that coaxes your own pituitary into releasing more GH could help frailty, wasting, and bone loss — that was the bet.

The trials

Tested in older adults for body composition, hip-fracture recovery, obesity, and Alzheimer's progression. There were genuine signals — lean mass, bone density, sleep — but no decisive win.

The stall

Development was discontinued without approval. Modest benefits plus blood-sugar increases and swelling never cleared the bar that regulators require.

The afterlife

The published trial data became forum lore. The compound lives on as an unregulated research chemical — the science is real, the context it's sold in is not.

Plain English

Imagine a promising rookie who played several real seasons (actual human trials), put up decent-but-not-great numbers, and never earned a contract (no approval). Years later, fans trade his rookie card like he was a star. The card is real. The stardom isn't.

03Mechanism

How it's supposed to work

The mechanism is unusually well-mapped — because MK-677 went through real drug development. Each step below was measured in humans, which is rare in this corner of the internet.

The least controversial part of MK-677: the pharmacology was measured, not guessed.

Oral and long-lived

It survives digestion and lingers about 24 hours (its half-life) — which is why study protocols used once-daily dosing. That's a pharmacology fact about the molecule, not an instruction.

Pulses, not a drip

It triggers natural-style pulses of growth hormone rather than a constant flood — mimicking the body's own rhythm was the design goal.

IGF-1 is a marker

Trials tracked IGF-1 because it's easy to measure in blood. A higher number is a signal the drug is active — not the same as a better health outcome.

Plain English

It's a forged hall pass: the pill looks enough like the hunger hormone to fool the receptor, the receptor pages the pituitary, and the pituitary releases growth hormone the way it would after a ghrelin surge. Clever forgery, real chain reaction — aimed at outcomes the trials never nailed down.

04Evidence

What the science actually supports

Unlike most internet-famous compounds, MK-677 has real human trials. The catch: they were small, short — and they ended without an approval.

Human trialsreal, but small and short
Elderly, obese, and Alzheimer's patients studied over weeks to ~2 years. Genuine data — and genuinely limited.
Phase 2 programscompleted, then stalled
Multiple indications explored (frailty, hip fracture, cognition). None advanced to approval.
Regulatory approvalnone, anywhere
Long-term safety dataessentially zero
No multi-year studies exist. What chronic use does over decades is uncharted.

What trials showed

Reliably raised GH and IGF-1, increased lean body mass, improved bone mineral density signals, and participants reported deeper sleep.

What trials didn't show

No proven fracture reduction, no meaningful strength gains, and the Alzheimer's program was stopped — biomarkers moved, outcomes didn't.

Why it stalled

Modest benefits weighed against rising blood sugar and swelling. The risk–benefit never cleared the bar regulators require.

Sleep was one of the more consistent subjective findings — and it fits the pharmacology.
The scale goes up — but the question is how much of that is water.

What people expect

Muscle growth, fat loss, and better recovery — the full growth-hormone fantasy in a pill. Forum lore often treats MK-677 as "HGH without the needle," promising visible physique changes within weeks. The appetite surge gets reframed as "bulking made easy," and the deeper sleep as proof it is working.

What research shows

Trials found higher GH and IGF-1, modest lean-mass gains, and deeper sleep — but no meaningful fat loss, no proven strength gains, and no fracture reduction. The Alzheimer's program was stopped. Benefits were real but modest; blood sugar rose and swelling appeared. Biomarkers moved; clinical outcomes largely did not.

Common effects & limits

Common: strong hunger, water retention, numb or tingly hands, lethargy, higher blood sugar. Limits: trials were small and short, no long-term safety data exists, and the scale cannot tell water from muscle. The long half-life means side effects linger as long as the benefits do.

Plain English

Picture a student who aced the quizzes (higher GH, IGF-1, lean mass) but failed the final exam (no meaningful clinical outcome, plus side effects). The school didn't expel him — they just never handed him the diploma. Real coursework, no degree.

05Reports

Forum reports: stories, not data

MK-677 has one of the largest anecdote piles in the gray-market scene — years of forum logs from people buying unregulated capsules. These are recurring themes, paraphrased: vivid, unverified, and never evidence.

Unverified anecdote — the pulsing dot marks a paraphrased recurring theme from forums and social media: not verified, not evidence.

Sleep like the dead

"Deepest sleep of my life — and the dreams are unreal." Caveat: sleep self-reports are notoriously placebo-prone, and nobody wore a sleep tracker. Some posters swear the vivid dreams arrived within the first week, describing nights that felt like being switched off and on again. The wry part: trial researchers did measure deeper slow-wave sleep, so this is one anecdote with lab-grade company — but "measured slow-wave minutes" and "dreams were unreal" are still two very different claims. Others mention a sleep hangover: groggy mornings that made the extra depth feel like a trade, not a gift. And a few wonder whether they simply slept better because they'd spent all day grazing.

Pattern: the most consistently reported effect — and the least objectively measured.

The hunger surge

"Ate everything in sight for the first two weeks straight." Caveat: a ghrelin mimic driving hunger is expected pharmacology — an effect, not a bonus. Some describe the hunger as unfamiliar — not the usual empty-stomach feeling but a gnawing urgency, like the body had moved dinnertime up by several hours. A few report raiding the fridge at hours they used to sleep through, joking that the grocery bill became the real side effect. The wry observation: it's the one effect nobody disputes, because it's literally the molecule's job description. Some say it faded after a few weeks; others say it never did — which, for a hunger-hormone impersonator, is more feature than bug.

Pattern: appetite increase is the drug working as designed, for better or worse.

Puffy and heavy

"Gained 8 pounds in a month — my face looked swollen." Caveat: rapid early weight gain is mostly water retention, not tissue. Some report wedding rings suddenly fitting tighter, or ankles carrying sock marks by evening. The wry part: the scale moving fast is exactly what makes it exciting — and exactly what makes it suspect, since tissue doesn't work that fast. A few mention the puffiness settling over time even as the scale stayed up, as if the water found a new equilibrium. Others admit they celebrated the weight until someone photographed them from the side.

Pattern: scale weight gets celebrated as "gains" when much of it is fluid.

Numb hands at night

"Kept waking up with tingling fingers." Caveat: matches the edema-related tingling seen in trials — and also matches sleeping on your arm. Some describe a buzzing pins-and-needles feeling that shows up in the early hours and fades once they're up and moving. It sits in a funny spot: common enough in trial data to be credible, ordinary enough in everyday life to be noise. A few mention shaking their hands out like a cartoon character and going straight back to sleep. Others report it never happening at all — the quiet control group that forgot to post.

Pattern: consistent with the swelling documented in actual studies.

The lethargy wall

"Felt sluggish and foggy every afternoon." Caveat: fatigue is commonly reported with GH-axis drugs, but also with overeating and bad sleep. Some posters call it the mid-afternoon shutdown — a heavy-lidded stretch of the day when coffee apparently stopped working. The wry wrinkle: the same drug celebrated for deep sleep gets blamed for groggy days, which suggests the sleep story has a second chapter. A few wonder whether the lethargy was really the extra calories and worse sleep schedule talking, rather than the capsule. Others describe it easing once they adjusted — better sleep habits, lighter evenings, the boring fixes. Nobody glamorizes this one; it's the anecdote pile's designated downer.

Pattern: the less-posted downside of the famous "deep sleep" story.

Blood sugar creep

"My fasting glucose was up at my checkup." Caveat: trials measured this too — it's the best-documented risk, and why diabetics are warned off. Some describe the moment as genuinely sobering — the first checkup number that turned the experiment from casual to serious. It's the one anecdote that arrives with receipts: the trials measured rising fasting glucose and HbA1c, so these stories land on pre-laid groundwork. A few mention their numbers sliding back after stopping, which is reassuring and also exactly what the trial data predicted. The wry truth: of all the effects people post about, this is the one doctors actually worry about — everything else is cosmetic or subjective, and this one is metabolic.

Pattern: the side effect with actual trial data behind it.

Nothing happened

"Two months in and I feel zero difference." Caveat: non-responders post less — and unregulated capsules vary in what they contain. These posts have a distinctive tone: not angry, just faintly embarrassed, like admitting the magic trick left them cold. Some wonder whether their capsules contained anything at all — a fair question in a market with no quality oversight. Others note their bloodwork never budged either, which at least settles the mystery with data instead of vibes. The wry observation: non-responders are the forum's invisible majority, too bored to log — and their reports are arguably the most valuable ones, the ones that never made the highlight reel.

Pattern: the quiet minority in every thread.

The long-run talk

"Everyone says you need to run it for months to see anything." Caveat: long unsupervised use is exactly what has no safety data — forum consensus isn't a study. This is the advice that shows up whenever someone asks why nothing is happening yet — patience, apparently, is the active ingredient. Some veterans describe timelines stretching half a year or more, always just beyond the reach of any actual data. The wry wrinkle: the longer the recommended run, the longer the unsupervised exposure nobody has studied. A few posters push back, pointing out that trials showed measurable GH and IGF-1 changes within weeks — so if nothing is moving, more months probably won't conjure it. Either way, "keep going" is free to say and not free to follow.

Pattern: duration advice with no evidence behind it.

Read these the way scientists read case reports: useful for generating hypotheses, useless for proving them. The most reported effects — hunger, water weight, sleep changes — are also the ones most tangled with expectation and lifestyle.

06Risks

Risks & unknowns

Because MK-677 went through real trials, its risk file is thicker than most — part observed, part expected, part unknown. Kept in separate boxes on purpose.

Observed in trials

Blood sugar rises

Measured: higher fasting glucose and HbA1c, reduced insulin sensitivity. People with diabetes or prediabetes face the clearest documented risk.

Observed in trials

Edema and tingling

Measured: water retention, swollen ankles, numb or tingling hands. Usually reversible — and commonly reported in forums too.

Expected pharmacology

Appetite and weight gain

Predictable: a ghrelin mimic drives hunger. Weight gained is part water, part fat — the celebrated "bulk" is not all muscle.

Unknown

Long-term safety

No multi-year human data. What chronic GH/IGF-1 elevation does over decades is uncharted — including theoretical concerns researchers have flagged but never tested.

Unknown

What's in the capsule

Unregulated product: gray-market capsules vary in content and dose. No quality oversight, no purity guarantees — the trial data may not describe what's actually sold.

Plain English

Known: it raises blood sugar, causes water retention, and tested athletes steer clear of it. Expected: relentless hunger. Unknown: what years of it do — and what's actually in the capsule. A thicker risk file than most, because real trials wrote part of it.

07A vs B

MK-677 vs the field

MK-677 is constantly compared to its relatives — but the comparisons that matter are specific: pill versus injection, 24 hours versus a brief pulse, ghrelin door versus GHRH door. Here is how it actually stacks up.

MK-677 vs Ipamorelin

Mechanism

Both knock on the ghrelin receptor (GHS-R1a) — same door, different keys. MK-677 is a small molecule; ipamorelin is a peptide.

Delivery

Pill vs injection. MK-677 survives digestion; ipamorelin does not and must bypass the gut.

Duration

~24 hours vs a few hours. One lingers all day; the other is a brief pulse, usually timed around sleep.

GH·IGF-1

Both raise GH and IGF-1. MK-677's longer presence means higher total daily exposure; ipamorelin gives a cleaner, shorter pulse.

Appetite

MK-677: strong hunger surge. Ipamorelin was designed to be the selective one — GH pulse with far less appetite.

Evidence

MK-677 has larger human trials (elderly, obese, Alzheimer's) but the programs stalled. Ipamorelin's human data is thinner.

Limitations

MK-677: blood-sugar rise, water retention, all-day hunger. Ipamorelin: thinner evidence, injection burden, shorter effect window.

Bottom line: The same receptor, opposite philosophies — all-day oral coverage versus a short, selective pulse.

MK-677 vs CJC-1295

Mechanism

Ghrelin mimic vs GHRH copy. MK-677 presses the hunger-hormone button; CJC-1295 copies the brain's own 'release GH' command (GHRH). Different receptors, same pituitary.

Delivery

Pill vs injection. CJC-1295 is a peptide — no oral route.

Duration

MK-677 lasts ~24h; CJC-1295 (no DAC) acts for hours. The DAC version stretches to days, flipping the duration advantage.

GH·IGF-1

Both raise GH/IGF-1. GHRH-path drugs tend to give bigger pulse amplitude; MK-677 gives steadier, longer exposure.

Appetite

MK-677: major hunger. CJC-1295: little to none — the GHRH path doesn't touch the appetite circuit.

Evidence

MK-677: real human trials, stalled programs. CJC-1295: human data exists but its development programs were discontinued.

Limitations

MK-677: metabolic costs (glucose, water, hunger). CJC-1295: injections, shorter-acting form needs frequent use, thin modern data.

Bottom line: Two different doors into the pituitary — pick your trade-off: appetite and convenience versus a cleaner, bigger pulse.

MK-677 vs CJC + Ipamorelin

Mechanism

One molecule vs a two-drug stack. The stack hits both doors at once — GHRH receptor (CJC) plus ghrelin receptor (ipamorelin) — for a synergistic pulse. MK-677 works the ghrelin door alone, all day.

Delivery

One pill vs two injections. The stack is the most burdensome regimen here; MK-677 the least.

Duration

Stack: short, timed pulses (usually around sleep). MK-677: 24-hour background presence — no timing strategy needed or possible.

GH·IGF-1

The stack is designed for maximum pulse height; MK-677 for maximum coverage. Different shapes of the same curve.

Appetite

Stack: mild (ipamorelin is the selective one, CJC adds none). MK-677: the hungriest option on this page.

Evidence

Stack: mostly community practice and small studies, not large trials. MK-677: larger formal trials, but for different indications.

Limitations

Stack: two drugs, two unknowns, injection burden, no long-term data. MK-677: metabolic side effects that never go away while it is active.

Bottom line: Maximum pulse versus maximum coverage — the stack chases height, the pill chases duration.

MK-677 vs HGH

Mechanism

Indirect vs direct. MK-677 asks your pituitary to make more GH; HGH (somatropin) is GH, injected directly, bypassing your control systems.

Delivery

Pill vs injection. HGH must be injected; it would be digested like any protein.

Duration

MK-677: ~24h of pulses preserving natural rhythm. HGH: direct blood levels that override the pulse pattern entirely.

GH·IGF-1

HGH gives precise, dose-proportional IGF-1 control. MK-677 gives whatever your pituitary decides to release — variable between people.

Appetite

HGH: no direct hunger effect. MK-677: strong appetite stimulation via the ghrelin pathway.

Evidence

HGH: decades of approved medical use with known benefits and risks. MK-677: real trials, no approval, programs stalled.

Limitations

HGH: shuts down natural production, prescription-only, well-known risk profile. MK-677: blood sugar, edema, and an evidence file that ends mid-story.

Bottom line: Asking the body versus telling it — one preserves the rhythm, the other replaces it.

MK-677 vs the rest

GHRP-6

The older ghrelin mimic — injectable, stronger hunger, and extra baggage (raises prolactin and cortisol). MK-677 is its cleaner, oral successor.

Sermorelin

A GHRH fragment — short-acting, brief GH nudge, historically used then largely superseded. Opposite corner: injectable, short, no appetite effect.

Tesamorelin

A GHRH analog with real approval (HIV lipodystrophy) and phase-3 data for visceral fat. The evidence heavyweight of the family; daily injection.

Ibutamoren's edge

Oral, long-acting, and genuinely studied in humans — a rare combination. No other ghrelin-path drug pairs a pill with 24-hour coverage.

Ibutamoren's cost

The same 24-hour coverage means 24-hour side effects — hunger, water, glucose — with no off-switch until it clears.

Bottom line: Same neighborhood, different streets — MK-677 owns the oral/long-acting corner, and pays for it in metabolic side effects.
Plain English

Think of the GH world as different vehicles. MK-677 is the comfortable sedan you drive all day — easy (a pill), always on, but it guzzles fuel (hunger, water, blood sugar). Ipamorelin is a sports car for short trips — fun pulse, then parked. CJC-1295 is a bigger engine for the same trip. HGH is a chauffeur — precise, but you are no longer driving. None of them is "the best" — they are answers to different questions.

08Benefits

Potential benefits

MK-677 has something most compounds in this space lack: real trial measurements. The tiers below separate what trials actually proved from what was predicted — and what was merely hoped for.

Established · trial data

Raised GH, IGF-1 — and appetite

Human trials reliably measured three things: higher growth-hormone pulses, higher IGF-1, and stronger appetite. These are genuine, replicated trial findings — the strongest “benefits” file of any compound in this series.

  • GH and IGF-1 rose across multiple trials
  • Appetite increased — the ghrelin-mimic signature
  • Lean-body-mass signals in frail elderly participants

Think of it this way: the trials proved the pill flips the right switches. But flipping switches was never the goal — building strength, preventing fractures, and slowing Alzheimer’s were. Those outcomes never materialized.

Emerging / predicted

Lean mass, sleep — signals, not verdicts

Trials noticed modest lean-mass gains in frail elderly participants (mixed results) and deeper slow-wave sleep reported by participants. Encouraging as directions — but modest, subjective in places, and never enough to carry an approval.

  • Lean mass up in some frailty-trial participants — water confounds the reading
  • Deeper sleep reported — consistent with bigger nighttime GH pulses
  • Bone-density signals — never translated into proven fracture reduction

Think of it this way: a body-composition scan cannot tell muscle from water — and MK-677 is famous for adding water. A bigger “lean mass” number is not automatically bigger muscles.

Community reports · stories, not data

What forums describe

The loudest anecdotes in this space — unverified, tangled with expectation and lifestyle changes, and sometimes reading trial side effects as success signals.

  • “Insane hunger within days” — matches the trial data, at least
  • “Best sleep of my life” — vivid, but self-reported and placebo-prone
  • “Scale weight shot up” — echoed widely; trials say much of it is water
  • “Gained real muscle” — the headline claim the trials could not confirm
09Who it suits

Who it may suit — and who it should not

MK-677 was studied in real patient populations — which makes the “who” unusually concrete. It also means the caution list comes from actual trial data, not theory.

May be relevant to

  • Frail and elderly adults — the core population of the phase-2 programs (frailty, hip fracture, muscle wasting)
  • Researchers of ghrelin biology — briefly studied in GH-deficient children; a real chapter of appetite-hormone science
  • Students of failed-drug case studies — one of the clearest examples of “good pharmacology, failed outcome”

May not suit / extra caution

  • People with diabetes or blood-sugar concerns — trials measured rising blood sugar; this is documented, not theoretical
  • Anyone prone to fluid retention — edema and puffiness were consistent trial findings; a heart-failure caution appeared in trial data
  • Tested athletes — banned by WADA, whatever the forum status debates say
  • Anyone expecting proven muscle or fat-loss results — the trials looked for them and did not find them

This is general information, not personal medical advice. Established indications: there are none — MK-677 was never approved for any use. The populations above are who researchers studied, not who should use it.

11Takeaways

The bottom line

Ten chapters, distilled. What the molecule is, what the evidence says, and what would have to change.

💊

A pill, not a peptide

MK-677 is a small-molecule drug — the single most misunderstood fact about it, and the reason it's orally active. It gets filed under "peptides" on forums by sheer proximity — sold on the same sites, discussed in the same threads, confused by the same newcomers. But chemistry doesn't care about shelf placement: a single compact molecule survives the gut, which is why the whole project was possible in the first place. Get this fact wrong and everything downstream — the appeal, the risks, the half-life — reads as mystery instead of mechanism. It's the correction the rest of the page depends on.

🍽️

Hunger-hormone mimic

It fools the ghrelin receptor → pituitary releases GH in pulses → liver makes IGF-1. A real, measured chain. Every link was measured in humans, which is genuinely rare for compounds with this kind of internet footprint. But a measured chain is not a proven benefit — it proves the forgery works, not that the heist was worth it. And the hunger lever comes free with the GH lever; there is no version of this signal that skips appetite.

⏱️

~24-hour half-life

A pharmacology fact: the drug lingers about a day, which is why study protocols used once-daily dosing. Not an instruction. Long-lived means forgiving of a missed day and unforgiving if side effects show up — you can't just wait an hour for it to clear. It's a property of the molecule, in the same category as its weight or color: interesting, neutral, and not advice. The "not an instruction" disclaimer exists because spec sheets keep getting read as invitations. Treat it like a fun fact, because that's what it is.

🧪

Real human trials

Tested for frailty, hip fracture, obesity, and Alzheimer's — small and short, but genuine human data. That puts it ahead of most internet-famous compounds, which usually top out at rodent studies and enthusiasm. But "real trials" cuts both ways: the trials also produced the blood-sugar data and the swelling reports. Having evidence means having all of it — including the parts the marketing skips.

📉

Stalled program

Modest benefits, blood-sugar and swelling costs. No regulator anywhere said yes. Programs don't stall from conspiracy; they stall from arithmetic — the benefits didn't outweigh the costs where it counted. The trials were the drug's best shot, run by people who wanted it to succeed. When even the home team benches a player, the box score told a story.

🩸

Known effects

Appetite, water retention, tingling, higher blood sugar, lethargy — documented in trials, echoed in forums. It's an unusually honest list, because the trial data and the forum chatter mostly agree — a rare consensus in this space. Notice what's missing: the headline benefits people hope for never made the confirmed list. The effects we know about are largely the costs, not the prizes. That asymmetry is the whole story in miniature.

💬

Stories aren't data

Sleep and hunger anecdotes are vivid and unverified, tangled with expectation and lifestyle. Vividness isn't a flaw — it's what makes anecdotes worth collecting, as raw material for hypotheses. The flaw is stopping there: mistaking a pile of stories for a conclusion. Every "best sleep of my life" post is data about what someone experienced; none of them is data about what the drug does. The distinction is the entire point of chapter 5.

🔭

What would change the story

A modern, long, well-controlled trial with a real clinical outcome. Until then: an interesting failed drug with a loud afterlife. Not a bigger forum thread, not a better anecdote — a trial, with controls, endpoints, and years. The bar is deliberately high, because the claims got loud while the evidence stayed small. Until someone clears it, the story stands: a real drug, a real program, a real ending — and an afterlife that outshouted all three. That's not a tragedy; it's just how the filter works.
